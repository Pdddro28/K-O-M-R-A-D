Electromechanical diagrams
====

When building an autonomous vehicle, understanding how and why your components talk to each other is just as important as the code you write. The Makeblock MegaPi serves as the physical muscle and sensory hub of our vehicle, handling the real-time processing that keeps the car moving precisely. Instead of stacking the MegaPi directly on top of the Raspberry Pi 4, we intentionally connect them via a standard USB serial cable. This decision is a lifesaver for space optimization within our chassis, allowing us to position the boards freely while establishing a robust data bridge. This setup ensures that high-level computer vision commands processed by OpenCV on the Raspberry Pi are instantly translated into low-level hardware actions on the MegaPi without risking any delicate circuitry during soldering.

To handle the primary propulsion of the vehicle, we connect our high-speed RS380 DC geared motor directly to Port 1 on the MegaPi. We selected this specific port because the MegaPi architecture features modular slots designed to house heavy-duty motor drivers right over these ports. By routing the motor lines into the green screw terminals of Port 1, we ensure that the high current demanded by the drive motor draws directly from our 11.1V LiPo battery. This architectural separation is crucial because it isolates the heavy electrical noise and power spikes of the motor away from the microcontroller's sensitive logic circuits, preventing our system from resetting during sudden acceleration bursts.

For steering control, our front wheels rely on the high-torque MG996R digital servo, which we wire into the designated pin headers on the upper right section of the MegaPi, utilizing a hardware-timed analog/PWM pin for the signal line. Steering an autonomous car using Ackermann geometry requires incredibly precise, jitter-free positioning, which can only be achieved by leveraging these dedicated pulse-width modulation pins. Connecting the servo to this specific row also grants it direct access to a stable five-volt power rail and common ground capable of handling the heavy current spikes that happen when the front wheels push against friction during sharp turns.

Finally, to give our vehicle complete situational awareness, we integrate three HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensors across the chassis and map them to distinct digital input/output pin channels on the MegaPi. Each of the three sensors requires its own unique Trigger and Echo pin pair so that the microcontroller can independently calculate distances for the left, center, and right pathways without any signal overlapping. By mapping them to these separate, clearly defined pin headers, the MegaPi can cleanly trigger a pulse and listen for the returning echo from each sensor in parallel, allowing our navigation software to make split-second steering adjustments when avoiding obstacles on the track.
